Part I of III

Beginning

images/lab.jpg — dusty university lab at dawn, empty chair, open window, corkboard of river maps (see Cast & Locations)

Yana Draganova was seven minutes late, which was six minutes later than Professor Vasileva ever tolerated, and she took the last flight of stairs to Room 214 two steps at a time, her sneakers slapping against worn stone that generations of engineering students had already worn smooth. The University of Ruse's main building always smelled the same in early morning — chalk dust, old radiator heat, and, faintly, the green mineral smell of the Danube that seeped in through every window left open overnight. She liked that smell. It reminded her that the river was never far, even inside these thick nineteenth-century walls.

She had her apology ready — the tram was late, I swear — but the words died before she said them, because the door to the Cybernetics and Signals Lab was already open, and it was never open this early.

The room was empty.

Not empty the way it was empty on a Sunday, lights off and chairs tucked in. Empty the way a room looks after someone has left it in a hurry. One of the tall arched windows stood ajar, its peeling frame letting in a thin ribbon of cold morning air that stirred the corkboard papers — river maps, sensor schematics, printouts of flood-level charts stretching back six years, all pinned in Vasileva's careful, colour-coded system. A chair at the long desk had been shoved back so hard it had left four black scuff marks on the tile. The professor's leather satchel, the one with the brass buckles she carried everywhere, was gone. Her coat was gone. Her coffee, though — her coffee was still there, cold, three-quarters full, in the chipped mug that said UNIVERSITY OF RUSE — 1945 in faded gold letters.

Yana stood in the doorway for a long moment, feeling something cold settle low in her stomach, a feeling she recognized and hated, because she'd felt it once before, nine years ago, on a night when the water had come up over the embankment faster than anyone said it could.

"Professor?" she said, to no one.

She made herself move. She checked the server racks — still humming, still logging, nothing touched. She checked Vasileva's desk drawers, and it was in the third one, half-hidden under a spare oscilloscope probe, that she found a single torn page, covered edge to edge in Vasileva's private cipher: rows of numbers and symbols that meant nothing to anyone else in the department, because Vasileva had built the cipher herself, twenty years ago, as a young researcher paranoid about her advisor stealing her notes. She had taught it to exactly one person. Yana slid the page into her pocket, fingers brushing, out of old habit, the small brass compass clipped inside — her father's, salt-stained, the glass cracked in one corner, the only thing of his that had come back to her after the flood.

She did not call the department office. She did not call security. She had learned, in three years as Vasileva's research assistant, that the professor trusted almost no one, and that whatever had happened here, the person who found it first needed to think very carefully about who to tell.

Instead she did what Vasileva had trained her to do with any anomaly: she documented before she disturbed anything. She photographed the scuff marks, the ajar window, the cold coffee, the exact angle of the chair. She noted that the corkboard's flood-level printouts had been rearranged — not removed, just shuffled, as though someone had gone through them quickly, looking for one specific page and not caring if the rest fell out of order. She noted, too, that the lock on the supply cabinet where Vasileva kept the buoy hardware had been forced, badly, by someone in a hurry who did not know the cabinet's second, hidden latch — which meant whoever had searched this room did not know the lab as well as they wanted people to think.

Only then did she call the one person who might not immediately report it upward: Dr. Ivan Genchev, the ancient, half-deaf lab technician who had kept a spare key to Room 214 since before Yana was born, and who agreed, grumbling, to say nothing to the department office until she asked him to.

By that evening, in the cramped, string-lit workshop in the dorm basement where her friends kept their salvaged equipment, Yana had the cipher half-broken and a headache blooming behind her eyes. "It's not random," she said, chalk dust on her knuckles from the whiteboard she'd dragged over. "It's coordinates. Old ones — pre-GPS grid references. She converted them by hand so no software would flag the pattern."

"Coordinates to where?" asked Bobby, who was eating cold pizza directly off the box lid.

Yana turned the laptop around. A single pin sat blinking on the satellite map, near the embankment on the south bank, close by the great grey arc of the Danube Bridge. An old brick shape, half-swallowed in reeds, that she recognized without needing the map to tell her: the abandoned pumping station, sealed since before she was born.

She thought about the Dean's assembly two days earlier, the way he'd shaken Vasileva's hand for the photographers and called Iskra "a proud new chapter for our university," his cufflinks — small silver gears — catching the light. She thought about how Vasileva had smiled for the camera and said nothing afterward, all the way back to the lab, her jaw tight in a way Yana hadn't understood until now.

"I'm going tonight," Yana said.

Bobby looked at her like she'd suggested swimming the Danube in December, which, this time of year, was not far from the truth. "Alone? To an abandoned Soviet pumping station? At night? Yana, if this is about the Dean —"

"It's not about the Dean," Yana said, too quickly, and Bobby's raised eyebrow told her he didn't believe it either. She softened. "Okay. It might be a little about the Dean. But it's mostly about her. She taught me everything I know about reading a system before it fails — noticing the small wrong thing before it becomes the big one. The window was open nine centimeters. Her coffee was exactly three sips gone. Someone came for her, or she left in a hurry because she thought someone was coming. Either way, she needs someone who can read her handwriting, and there's exactly one person on this campus who can."

"Take my bike light, at least," Bobby said, which was as close as he came to admitting he couldn't talk her out of it.
