End

symposium.jpg — stage reveal, two glowing screens, Dean rising in alarm (see Cast & Locations)

The University Symposium Hall filled that Friday the way it always did for the annual research showcase — polite applause, department heads in the front rows, a scattering of local journalists hoping for a quote. Dean Stoykov stood near the podium in a navy suit, gear-shaped cufflinks catching the light exactly as they had the day Iskra was first announced, entirely unaware that the program had changed.

When Vasileva walked out onto the stage — presumed by half the room to have been on medical leave, according to the story the Dean's office had quietly circulated — a ripple of murmurs crossed the hall. Stoykov's smile did not waver, but Yana, standing at the side controls with Mitko and Georgi in the front row, saw his hand tighten almost imperceptibly on the edge of the podium.

"Six years ago," Vasileva began, "my lab began building a system to predict flooding on the Danube before it happens. Tonight I want to show you what it can do — and what it already showed us, nine years in the past."

The first screen lit with Iskra's live feed: real-time river data, a soft blue model of the current flowing steadily beneath the Danube Bridge, a rising amber line at the edge of the chart that meant, in plain terms, that a flood surge was already building upstream, expected to reach Ruse within thirty-six hours — sooner than any official forecast had predicted.

The second screen lit with a scanned ledger page, a downgraded sealant specification, and an initialed signature that half the room recognized immediately, because they had seen it on a hundred memos.

Stoykov started forward, something sharp forming behind his composed expression — "This is completely unauthorized, this material was sealed, I must insist —" His voice caught on the word insist, as though he'd expected it to land the way it always had in faculty meetings, and found instead that the room had already stopped listening to him. A murmur moved through the rows like wind through reeds. In the third row, a woman from the city's water authority had gone very still, phone half-raised to photograph the screen. Near the back, a journalist Yana recognized from the local paper was already typing.

"This material," Vasileva said, not raising her voice at all, which somehow carried further than shouting would have, "was sealed by the same office that approved the contract on the screen behind me. I don't think that's a coincidence, and I don't think anyone in this room does either."

The room was already reading the dates for itself, and Yana, standing at the side controls, had made sure copies had gone out an hour earlier — to three journalists, to the city's emergency management office, and to the rector directly, bypassing the Dean's office entirely. There was no version of this evening where it stayed sealed any longer.

"You had the chance to fix the barrier honestly," Vasileva said, quiet enough that the microphone made it sound like she was speaking to him alone, though every person in the hall heard it. "Instead you buried a mistake, and it cost lives. I built Iskra so it could never happen again. Whether it costs you your position is not my concern. Whether it saves this city in thirty-six hours is."

It did.

The emergency crews began reinforcing the embankment that night, working from Iskra's precise surge predictions instead of guesswork, and when the Danube rose thirty-six hours later — high and fast and exactly on the hour Iskra had forecast — it rose against barriers that held, and against a town that had been given time to prepare instead of caught by surprise. Sandbags where the model said the water would push hardest. Evacuation of the low-lying student dormitory a full day early. No one died. It was, the local paper wrote the following week, the first time in a generation that the Danube had risen and nobody in Ruse had needed to hold a funeral afterward.

Stoykov resigned before the disciplinary committee finished its first session; the sealant contract became, quietly, the subject of a criminal referral Yana did not need to follow to know how it would likely end. Vasileva was reinstated with her research budget not just restored but doubled, though she waved off the university's proposed press conference with the same dry irritation she'd shown for cameras from the beginning.

On the evening after the water finally receded, Yana found her at the embankment near the old pumping station, watching the brown water settle back into its ordinary banks, Georgi's coat still around her shoulders because, she said, it was warmer than her own.

"You could have told me," Yana said. "About the contract. About my father."

"I didn't know for certain until three weeks before I disappeared," Vasileva said. "And once I knew, I was afraid that telling you would make you reckless before I could make it safe to be reckless." She looked over, something almost gentle crossing her guarded features. "I was wrong about the second part. You were reckless regardless. You were also right."

Yana laughed, unsteady, and closed her hand around her father's compass, its glass still cracked, its needle still true. For nine years the river had been the place she went to grieve. Standing there now, watching the water settle calm and low beneath the bridge, she thought that maybe, from now on, it could also be the place she went to remember that some things, once broken, could still be repaired in time — if someone was stubborn enough to look for the crack before it split all the way through.

Behind them, up on the hill, the lights of the University of Ruse burned steady against the evening, and in Room 214, the servers kept humming, kept listening, kept watching the river the way Vasileva had taught them to: patiently, precisely, and never once looking away.

THE END
