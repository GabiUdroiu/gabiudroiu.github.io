Middle

pumping-station.jpg — foggy Soviet-era pumping station on the Danube riverbank, new padlock on an old iron door (see Cast & Locations)

The old pumping station smelled of rust and standing water, and the fog off the river had thickened enough that the Danube Bridge behind it was only a suggestion of lights, blurred and distant. Yana's flashlight found the iron door first — and the padlock on it, which was wrong, because everything else on this building was fifty years old and eaten through with rust, and the padlock was new, its steel bright and unweathered.

She had a key. Not a copy — the actual key, small and brass, taped flat along the last fold of the cipher page all along, hidden beneath a smear of dried candle wax she'd mistaken for an ink blot.

Inside, her flashlight beam slid over corroded pipe housings and a floor slick with condensation, until it found, in the center of the room, a folding table that did not belong to 1962: a laptop running on a car battery, a spool of waterproof cable running out through a drilled hole toward the river, and, cradled in a padded case, a sleek buoy prototype no bigger than a beach ball, stenciled with a single word — ISKRA — in Vasileva's own handwriting.

"You shouldn't be here."

Yana spun. A man stood in the doorway, broad-shouldered, dressed for weather rather than for the university, a coil of rope over one shoulder. Not Vasileva. Older, gruffer, a face she almost recognized from somewhere near the docks.

"Neither should you," Yana said, with more steadiness than she felt, her hand closing around the cold brass compass in her pocket.

He didn't move toward her. He looked, instead, past her, at the buoy, and something in his expression eased. "You're the assistant. Draganova." He said her name carefully, like he'd practiced it. "She talks about you. Said you'd be smart enough to find this, and stubborn enough not to stop."

"Where is she?"

"Safe. For now." He crouched, checking the cable connection with the ease of someone who had done it many times before. "She asked me to keep an eye on the station. Make sure nobody from the Dean's office got here first."

Over the following days, Yana learned his name was Georgi, a former river-lock engineer Vasileva had trusted since her earliest fieldwork on the Danube, long before Iskra had a name. And she learned, in careful fragments, why her mentor had vanished: three weeks earlier, Vasileva had run Iskra's predictive model against six years of real sensor data and gotten a result that terrified her — not a forecast of a future flood, but proof that the flood nine years ago, the one that took Yana's father and eleven others, should never have overwhelmed the embankment at all. The barrier reinforcement contract the university had signed off on that year — approved, ultimately, by a junior administrator who was now the Dean — had used substandard sealant to cut costs. Iskra's model, run backward, showed it plainly: the barrier should have held.

Vasileva had taken the analysis to Stoykov first, privately, as a courtesy. Two days later, her office had been searched while she was at lunch. Nothing was taken. It was a message.

So she disappeared before he could make her disappear more permanently from the record — before her data could be quietly declared "inconclusive," her funding pulled, her results buried the way, Yana now understood with a slow, sick anger, so much else at this university had been buried before.

The archive was Georgi's idea. "If she's right that Stoykov approved that contract," he said, "there's a paper trail. Universities never destroy paper. They just bury it."

Fund 9 was a warren of steel shelving under the main building, reached through a service door that hadn't been repainted since the Zhivkov era, lit by bulbs on pull-chains that buzzed like trapped flies. It took Yana and a sympathetic archivist named Mitko three evenings of quiet searching before they found the shelf — one shelf among hundreds, dusted suspiciously clean, its neighbors thick with a decade of undisturbed grime. Behind a loose panel where the shelf's end should have met the wall, Mitko's flashlight found a second, narrower room: the original 1962 contract file, complete with a sealant specification downgraded in someone's handwriting, initialed with the flourish that, nine years later, would sign Stoykov's letters as Dean.

"He's had this destroyed everywhere except here," Mitko whispered. "He must not know Fund 9 still has the original intake ledger."

Yana photographed every page with hands that would not quite stop shaking. Mitko stood watch at the service door, an old university keyring looped nervously around one finger, while the buzzing pull-chain bulb overhead threw long shadows across the ledger's brittle pages. Somewhere above them, footsteps crossed the main hall floor — a night guard, probably, or nothing at all — and both of them went still until the sound passed.

"My grandmother worked in this archive," Mitko whispered, once it was safe to breathe again. "She used to say paper doesn't lie, it just waits for someone patient enough to ask it the right question." He tapped the sealant specification with one finger, careful not to smudge sixty-year-old ink. "Looks like your professor finally asked."

They almost didn't make it out clean. On their second visit to Fund 9, headlights swept across the courtyard windows above them at an hour when no university vehicle had any business being there, and Yana killed her flashlight on instinct, pulling Mitko back behind the false shelf panel. Through the gap she watched two men in plain jackets try the service door, find it locked, and move on toward the lab wing instead — unhurried, methodical, the way people move when they are being paid by the hour rather than driven by urgency. She recognized one of them from the press photo of the Dean's assembly: the same broad shoulders as the "security consultant" the Dean's office had quietly added to campus rounds the week Iskra was supposed to launch. It was the first solid proof that whatever was happening to Vasileva, it was not paranoia. Someone with resources was actively searching for exactly what Yana was holding in her hands.

It was two nights later, back at the pumping station with the archive photos backed up in three places, that Georgi's radio — an old analog set clipped to his rope coil — crackled with a warning from a friend at the security firm the Dean had hired: they know about the station, moving on it tonight.

"Get the drive," Georgi said, already moving to unplug the buoy's data cable. "I'll hold the door."

That was when Vasileva stepped out from the narrow stairwell behind the old control panel — thinner than Yana remembered, her charcoal blazer replaced by one of Georgi's spare coats, but her eyes as sharp as ever.

"You were supposed to stay hidden," Yana said, half a laugh, half a sob rising in her throat.

"You were supposed to stay away from all this," Vasileva said. "We're both disappointing, it seems." She gripped Yana's shoulder once, hard, the closest thing to an embrace the professor had ever offered anyone. "Now. Did you bring the archive?"
